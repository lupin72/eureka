## ideas/fractionation.md

### 1. Parcel count on the wall

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Realised, it turns the 1,200 Facebook followers, the audience most likely to include under-50s, into weekday crew members with a named session and a visible result, so it grows the rota toward 40 and shifts its age mix. Single-session sign-ups only reach the Desired state if crews keep their newcomers, and a named crew with a weekly tally gives them a reason to come back.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each Tuesday–Friday sorting crew posts its parcel tally and a shelf photo with first names that evening, followers book single named sessions in one tap, and returning newcomers fill the weekday crews.
2. A free booking link (a Facebook event per session or a free form) lists each weekday session by crew name, and new bookers are sent to a grouped food-safety briefing before their first session.
3. One regular per crew has editor access to the Facebook page and agrees to post the tally and photo after the session, with crew members' consent to appear by first name.
4. The coordinator sets up the booking link and a monthly group briefing slot, and asks one regular per weekday crew to be its poster. This fits in the four spare hours a week, costs nothing from the €3,000, and uses the warehouse's existing Tuesday–Saturday hours and the existing page, so it can be done today.

### 2. The briefing is the first shift

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Realised, it turns more sign-ups into rota members by making the first visit count, which helps close the gap between eight lost and two recruited. It does not bring in new or younger people on its own, so it gets to the Desired state only alongside something that fills the funnel.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every newcomer's first visit is a paired two-hour sorting shift with a regular, and finishing it counts as passing the food-safety briefing, so they are on the rota from day one.
2. The briefing's content is turned into hands-on checkpoints that a regular teaches and signs off during sorting, while the newcomer handles food from the start of the shift.
3. Whoever owns the briefing requirement (Second Shelf's own policy, or its food-safety obligations) accepts teaching during the shift as the briefing, and a few regulars are trained to deliver and sign it off.
4. The coordinator has the current briefing material and a few willing regulars, and can rework it and train them over a few weeks within the four spare hours a week. This can be done today.

**Broken link:** Step 2: volunteers must pass the food-safety briefing, and here the newcomer handles food before passing it, because the briefing is only "passed" at the end of the shift.
**Possible fix:** Open the paired first shift with the short briefing itself, delivered by the trained regular and signed off before the newcomer touches open food. The newcomer spends those minutes on non-food tasks (boxes, labels, shelving), so the first visit is still a real shift and the constraint holds.
