# Verification

## ideas/random-entry.md

### 1. Graded runs

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** A one-slot-a-month green run with no lifting is a commitment working-age people can fit around jobs and families, so recruitment can outpace losses and bring in the under-50s the Desired state asks for. More small commitments still count as active volunteers on the rota.
**Backward chain:**

1. Second Shelf's rota runs on graded tasks: newcomers fill green slots, and some move up to blue and black by choice.
2. A first intake of newcomers has signed up for green runs, passed the food-safety briefing and been shadowed by existing blue or black volunteers.
3. The graded task list is published on the Facebook page, in the parish newsletter and on posters in the two partner supermarkets, with "two hours a month, no lifting" as the headline.
4. The coordinator has sorted every Saturday and warehouse task into green, blue and black and asked current regulars which grade they hold. This takes a few of the four spare hours and costs nothing, so it is achievable today.

### 2. Season pass cohorts

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** A free, bounded run of eight Saturdays is an easier yes for younger people than open-ended volunteering, and the renew-or-leave choice at each season end lets the rota be rebuilt four times a year towards 40 volunteers. Batching onboarding keeps the growth within the coordinator's time.
**Backward chain:**

1. Four cohorts a year start on set Saturdays. Each does the food-safety briefing together on day one, and at season end members renew, upgrade or leave with thanks.
2. The first cohort has signed up for a dated eight-Saturday season, and the coordinator has planned that season's rota around them.
3. The first season's start date and sign-up form are promoted on the Facebook page, in the parish newsletter and in the partner supermarkets.
4. The coordinator has picked the four start dates and set up a free sign-up form and a group briefing session. This fits the four spare hours and uses no budget, so it is achievable today.

### 3. Ski-school credentials

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** A recognised food-hygiene certificate, a reference letter and logged work hours are things job-seekers, students and career-changers need, almost all of them under 50. That gives them a reason to join and finish a full season, which feeds both the headcount and the under-50 share in the Desired state.
**Backward chain:**

1. Volunteers who complete a season leave with a recognised food-hygiene certificate, a reference letter and a log of their hours in warehouse logistics or van work.
2. The certificates are bought through an accredited online food-hygiene course at roughly €15–30 per person, so about 50 certificates use €750–1,500 of the €3,000 budget. A training cost is not payment, so it stays clear of "paying volunteers".
3. The coordinator has a reference-letter template and a simple hours log, so season-end paperwork takes minutes per volunteer and fits in the four spare hours.
4. The offer is pitched through the Facebook page and to local job centres, colleges and the parish, all reachable with existing channels and relationships.
5. The coordinator has chosen an accredited course provider and drafted the template. This is desk work within the four spare hours, so it is achievable today.

### 4. Bindings that release

**Verdict:** ❌ Not adherent
**Adherence:** With pauses and seated roles in place, fewer of the 30 over-65 regulars leave, so the rota may hold near 40. But nothing in the idea brings in under-50s, so the Desired state's "at least a third under 50" is missed and the ageing that causes the losses goes on.
**Backward chain:**

1. Any volunteer can pause for up to six months without leaving or redoing the briefing, and older regulars who can no longer lift work in seated roles.
2. Seated roles (intake desk, phoning households, stock lists) are defined and set up at the warehouse.
3. The coordinator keeps a pause register and a check-in reminder before each pause ends.
4. The coordinator has written down the release option and the seated roles and told the regulars. This needs no budget and fits the four spare hours, so it is achievable today.

**Actually solves:** Keeping existing, mostly older volunteers through illness, caring duties and declining strength, which slows the losses but does not renew the rota's age profile.

### 5. Skis come in pairs

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** If every over-65 regular hands their role to a younger partner, the rota keeps its know-how and changes its age profile as the older generation steps back, which delivers both the headcount and the under-50 share.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each over-65 regular works alongside a younger partner for a season, and when an older volunteer steps back, the partner takes over the role.
2. Around 30 younger partners have joined, passed the food-safety briefing, and committed to the same station and shift as their senior partner for a season.
3. Those 30 younger volunteers have been recruited.
4. The coordinator has matched each over-65 regular to a role they can pass on and listed the openings.

**Broken link:** Step 3. Recruiting around 30 under-50s is the problem itself: the current channels (Facebook page, parish, supermarkets) brought in two volunteers last year, and the coordinator's four hours a week cannot run a recruitment campaign that large.
**Possible fix:** Have each over-65 regular find their own partner among younger family, neighbours or parish contacts, with the coordinator only running the briefing and confirming the match. Recruitment then runs through 30 personal networks and not through the coordinator.

### 6. Après-shelf lunch

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** A weekly shared lunch makes the Saturday shift socially rewarding, which helps keep people, and the bring-a-guest rule recruits through personal networks, which tend to convert better than broadcast appeals. Together these address both sides of the losses-versus-recruits gap.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every Saturday distribution ends with a lunch for the volunteers who worked and one guest each, and some guests come back as volunteers.
2. A hot lunch for perhaps 20–40 people is cooked in the warehouse every Saturday from that day's surplus.
3. The warehouse has a kitchen that can legally prepare hot food for groups, and the supermarkets' donation terms allow surplus to feed volunteers and guests.
4. The coordinator has checked the warehouse facilities and the donation agreements.

**Broken link:** Step 3. The brief does not say the warehouse has a kitchen, and cooking for groups usually needs kitchen facilities and food-safety registration beyond the volunteers' short briefing. Feeding volunteers from donations meant for 220 households may also break the supermarkets' terms or their trust.
**Possible fix:** Hold the lunch in the partner parish's hall kitchen and pay for simple food from the budget (about €40 a week, roughly €2,000 a year), so donated food stays with households and no new kitchen is needed.

### 7. Group lift bookings

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Running clubs, five-a-side teams, parents' groups and workplace teams are mostly under 50 and arrive several people at a time, so a handful of groups can add the 20 or so volunteers needed and push the under-50 share past a third. One contact per group keeps the coordinator within four hours a week.
**Backward chain:**

1. Several local groups each staff one station on one Saturday a month as a crew and handle their own swaps.
2. Each crew member is over 18 and has passed the food-safety briefing, which the coordinator runs once per group.
3. Four or five groups have agreed to a monthly Saturday booking, each with one named contact.
4. The coordinator has pitched "book a lift" to local clubs, parents' groups and workplaces through the Facebook page, the parish and the staff of the two partner supermarkets.
5. The coordinator has written a one-page offer listing the stations groups can take over. This uses existing channels, costs nothing and fits the four spare hours, so it is achievable today.

## ideas/provocation.md

### 1. Neighbour shifts for parcel households

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. The 220 households include working-age adults who are there every Saturday, so turning some of them into regular volunteers adds under-50s to the rota and helps close the gap between volunteers lost and volunteers recruited.
**Backward chain:**

1. A group of adults from parcel households hold regular Saturday or warehouse slots, and several of them are under 50.
2. Those who said yes are over 18, have passed the short food-safety briefing and have been given a regular slot.
3. Households have been invited in a way that is clearly opt-in: a note in the parcel and a word in the queue saying that a "neighbour shift" has no effect on their parcel.
4. The coordinator writes the invitation note and prints it for the next Saturday's parcels, which costs very little and fits in their four spare hours. This is achievable today.

### 2. Turn up first, sign up later

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. It turns first shifts into regular slots, which raises recruitment above two a year. Walk-ins brought in through the Facebook page skew younger than the current rota, so it moves Second Shelf towards both 40 volunteers and the under-50 share.
**Backward chain:**

1. Newcomers who worked a supervised first Saturday shift accept a regular slot, and the rota grows.
2. Each walk-in at 8:30 has an ID check to confirm they are over 18, passes the short food-safety briefing on the spot and is paired with an experienced regular.
3. A briefing script and a short quiz or checklist exist, and one or two regulars are trained to give them every Saturday, so the briefing does not depend on the coordinator.
4. The "just turn up at 8:30" offer is posted on the Facebook page (1,200 followers) and in the parish newsletter.
5. The coordinator writes the briefing script and the post and asks two regulars to act as buddies, within four spare hours and at no cost. This is achievable today.

### 3. Supermarket staff on the sorting shift

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Mostly. Supermarket staff are mostly working-age, so they add under-50 hands. Released staff do count as volunteers as long as the employer, not Second Shelf, pays for their time. The weekday sorting hours alone do not make a stable Saturday rota, though. Only the staff who move on to Saturdays, or a fixed weekday team, count towards the stable 40.
**Backward chain:**

1. Supermarket staff sort donations at the warehouse on weekday afternoons, and some have joined the Saturday rota in their own time.
2. Each staff member has passed the food-safety briefing and is supervised during the sorting shift.
3. Each supermarket releases groups of staff for warehouse shifts as employer-supported volunteering hours.
4. Store or regional managers at the two supermarkets have agreed to count those hours as volunteering or team-building time.
5. The coordinator asks the supermarket contacts they already know whether an employer-volunteering scheme exists. This is achievable today.

**Broken link:** Step 2. If the staff rotate from group to group, every new group needs its own food-safety briefing and someone to supervise it on a weekday afternoon. That cannot fit in the coordinator's four spare hours a week.
**Possible fix:** Ask each supermarket for a small fixed team (the same three or four people on a regular weekly slot), so that each person is briefed only once. Have a trained regular volunteer lead the weekday sorting shift.

### 4. After-work warehouse evenings

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. Evening slots open the rota to working people under 50 who cannot help during the day, and Saturday distribution stays as it is.
**Backward chain:**

1. One or two evening slots (18:00–20:00) run every week for sorting, packing and date-checking, staffed largely by people with day jobs.
2. Evening volunteers are over 18, have passed the food-safety briefing and have signed up for the evening slots.
3. Someone with keys and food-safety responsibility opens, supervises and locks the warehouse on each evening slot.
4. The evening slots are announced on the Facebook page and in the parish newsletter, with an evening briefing for new joiners.
5. The coordinator chooses which weekday tasks can move to evenings and picks the evenings. This is achievable today.

**Broken link:** Step 3. If the coordinator has to supervise two 2-hour evenings a week, that uses all four spare hours, which leaves nothing for briefings, recruitment or anything else.
**Possible fix:** Start with one evening a week, run by one or two trusted regular volunteers who are given keys and trained to supervise, so that it takes none of the coordinator's hours once it is set up.

### 5. Ninety-minute micro-slots

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. Single 90-minute slots lower the commitment that keeps busy younger people away, while regulars can still stack slots. That adds active volunteers, including under-50s, to a Saturday rota.
**Backward chain:**

1. The Saturday rota is filled in 90-minute slots. Newcomers book one at a time and regulars stack several.
2. Newcomers pass the food-safety briefing at the start of their first slot and confirm they are over 18 when they book.
3. A free shared sign-up sheet (for example, an online spreadsheet or form) lists the slots, is linked from the Facebook page and is announced to its 1,200 followers.
4. Each Saturday task has a clear handover at slot changes, so that a person who stays only 90 minutes is useful.
5. The coordinator splits the Saturday morning into slots and sets up the free sheet within four spare hours. This is achievable today.

## ideas/challenge.md

### 1. Self-run swap board with shift captains

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. Once it is running, Saturdays are covered by a pool of at least 40 people who each give as many Saturdays as suits them. That deals with both halves of the Problem: fewer people leave from burnout or guilt over absences, and people under 50 with irregular working and family weeks, who cannot promise a fixed weekly slot, can now join. The pool can grow to the Desired state's 40 active volunteers, a third of them under 50.
**Backward chain:**

1. A year on, at least 40 active volunteers, at least a third of them under 50, claim and swap Saturday shifts on a shared board. A rotating volunteer captain checks coverage each week, and the coordinator is no longer the rota's bottleneck.
2. Before that, younger people have joined because the offer is "give one Saturday a month, or whenever suits you". Each new joiner has passed the short food-safety briefing before the board lets them claim a shift, which keeps the over-18 and briefing rule.
3. Before that, the flexible offer has been advertised on the 1,200-follower Facebook page, in the parish newsletter and in the two partner supermarkets. Short group briefings run on a weekday at the warehouse, which is open Tuesday to Saturday, so new joiners can start without adding to the coordinator's Saturday load.
4. Before that, the existing 45 regulars use the board for a trial period of a few Saturdays. Volunteers who do not use it online have a paper copy at the warehouse, and the week's captain phones them, so the over-65 majority is not shut out.
5. Before that, a few trusted regulars have agreed to take turns as shift captain. Each has a one-page checklist: confirm coverage by Thursday, fill any gaps from the board's reserve list, and greet any newcomer.
6. Before that, the coordinator has set up a free or low-cost shift board (a shared sign-up tool or spreadsheet) with each Saturday's roles. They do it in their four spare hours a week, at little or no cost to the €3,000 budget. This is achievable today.

## ideas/reversal.md

### 1. The briefing goes on tour

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. Offices and colleges are full of under-50s, so bringing the briefing to them adds younger people to the rota, which is the gap between today's 45 mostly over-65 regulars and a stable 40 with a third under 50.
**Backward chain:**

1. Groups in offices, colleges and the parish hall have sat the food-safety briefing at lunchtime, hold "cleared to volunteer" cards, and are booking first Saturday shifts through a QR link.
2. Every attendee who gets a card is cleared to volunteer, including the college students.
3. Host offices, colleges and the parish have agreed to lunchtime slots, and the cards and a QR link to a shift-booking form are printed and set up for a few hundred euros at most, well inside the €3,000 budget.
4. A retired veteran volunteer, free on weekdays, has been trained by the coordinator to deliver the short briefing, and the coordinator books the talks within the four spare hours a week.
5. The coordinator asks the parish, which Second Shelf already has good relations with, to host the first session, and approaches one or two local employers and colleges. This can be done today.

**Broken link:** Step 2. Many college students are under 18, and handing out cards to a whole group breaks the rule that volunteers must be over 18.
**Possible fix:** Check age when people book or when the card is issued, and give "cleared to volunteer" cards only to attendees confirmed to be 18 or over. At colleges, offer the session only to adult or final-year students.

### 2. The newcomer runs the Facebook page

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. Clips made by young peers about real Saturdays reach under-50s in a way that recruitment calls don't, so the idea works on the under-50 share and the size of the rota.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each quarter the newest under-30 volunteer runs the Facebook page and posts short behind-the-scenes clips of Saturdays, with the veterans as the stars, and younger viewers sign up.
2. Every quarter there is a new volunteer under 30 who is willing to take over the page.
3. The veterans and any households that appear in the clips have agreed to be filmed under a simple consent and privacy rule, such as never showing a recipient's face without consent.
4. The coordinator has written a one-page posting guide and set up a handover of admin access, using a few of the four spare hours a week and no budget.
5. The coordinator hands the page to the youngest current volunteer. This can be done today.

**Broken link:** Step 2. Second Shelf recruited only two volunteers last year, so nothing in the current situation guarantees a new under-30 volunteer every quarter. The idea relies on the inflow it is meant to create.
**Possible fix:** Give the page to the youngest active volunteer, whatever their age, and keep the role for a set term rather than tying it to the latest under-30 arrival. Pass it to a new under-30 volunteer once one joins.

## ideas/concept-fan.md

### 1. Run to the Shelf

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. A running club that turns up as a group every Saturday adds a recurring block of mostly under-50 volunteers to the distribution rota. That moves Second Shelf towards 40 active volunteers with at least a third under 50, and the weekly ritual makes them stay, which is what keeps the rota stable.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every Saturday a local running club's social run finishes at the warehouse. The runners do a 90-minute distribution shift and then share a breakfast.
2. A breakfast for the whole group is supplied and paid for every Saturday of the year.
3. The participating runners are all over 18, have passed the food-safety briefing in one group session run by the coordinator, and are on the Saturday rota as a named group.
4. The club has agreed to make the shift part of its weekly Saturday run. It has routed the run to end at the warehouse and timed it to match distribution hours.
5. The coordinator has met a local running club with a written proposal, using part of their four spare hours a week. The parish or the Facebook page can supply the contact. This is achievable today.

**Broken link:** Step 2 breaks the €3,000 annual budget. A weekly breakfast for a club group costs, for example, 20 runners × €3 × 52 Saturdays ≈ €3,100. That is more than the whole year's budget and leaves nothing for briefings or anything else.
**Possible fix:** Ask one of the two partner supermarkets to donate the breakfast items each Saturday as a sponsored "Run to the Shelf" breakfast. Then the budget pays only for occasional extras.

## ideas/fractionation.md

### 1. Parcel count on the wall

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Realised, it turns the 1,200 Facebook followers, the audience most likely to include under-50s, into weekday crew members with a named session and a visible result, so it grows the rota toward 40 and shifts its age mix. Single-session sign-ups only reach the Desired state if crews keep their newcomers, and a named crew with a weekly tally gives them a reason to come back.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each Tuesday–Friday sorting crew posts its parcel tally and a shelf photo with first names that evening, followers book single named sessions in one tap, and returning newcomers fill the weekday crews.
2. A free booking link (a Facebook event per session or a free form) lists each weekday session by crew name, and new bookers are sent to a grouped food-safety briefing before their first session.
3. One regular per crew has editor access to the Facebook page and agrees to post the tally and photo after the session, with crew members' consent to appear by first name.
4. The coordinator sets up the booking link and a monthly group briefing slot, and asks one regular per weekday crew to be its poster. This fits in the four spare hours a week, costs nothing from the €3,000, and uses the warehouse's existing Tuesday–Saturday hours and the existing page, so it can be done today.

### 2. The briefing is the first shift

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Realised, it turns more sign-ups into rota members by making the first visit count, which helps close the gap between eight lost and two recruited. It does not bring in new or younger people on its own, so it gets to the Desired state only alongside something that fills the funnel.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every newcomer's first visit is a paired two-hour sorting shift with a regular, and finishing it counts as passing the food-safety briefing, so they are on the rota from day one.
2. The briefing's content is turned into hands-on checkpoints that a regular teaches and signs off during sorting, while the newcomer handles food from the start of the shift.
3. Whoever owns the briefing requirement (Second Shelf's own policy, or its food-safety obligations) accepts teaching during the shift as the briefing, and a few regulars are trained to deliver and sign it off.
4. The coordinator has the current briefing material and a few willing regulars, and can rework it and train them over a few weeks within the four spare hours a week. This can be done today.

**Broken link:** Step 2: volunteers must pass the food-safety briefing, and here the newcomer handles food before passing it, because the briefing is only "passed" at the end of the shift.
**Possible fix:** Open the paired first shift with the short briefing itself, delivered by the trained regular and signed off before the newcomer touches open food. The newcomer spends those minutes on non-food tasks (boxes, labels, shelving), so the first visit is still a real shift and the constraint holds.

## ideas/alternatives.md

### 1. Saturday crèche, run by the elders

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. A Saturday childcare corner removes the main barrier for parents under 50, the group the brief's Desired state needs, and moving older regulars into a lighter role cuts the losses that shrink the rota.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every Saturday a supervised children's corner runs at the warehouse, staffed in turns by over-65 regulars, and parents volunteer at the packing and handout stations while their children are there.
2. A small group of older regulars has agreed to take turns, has had the food-safety briefing plus a short safeguarding briefing, and knows the rule that a parent stays on site the whole time, so it is supervision and not registered childcare.
3. A corner of the warehouse away from the van bay and pallet area is fenced off and stocked with mats, books, colouring materials and a snack table, and the insurer has confirmed that the public liability cover includes children on the premises. This costs a few hundred euros of the €3,000.
4. The coordinator, in two or three weeks of spare hours, has written a one-page safeguarding and children's corner policy and asked older regulars at a Saturday handout which of them would rather read stories than lift crates.
5. Today: the coordinator rings the insurer and walks the warehouse to pick the corner. Both fit in this week's four spare hours.

### 2. Bring-a-grandchild generations shift

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. If quarterly generations Saturdays turn invited relatives and neighbours into regulars, the rota grows with younger people and older volunteers pass on what they know before they step back, which is the renewal the brief asks for.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each quarter a generations Saturday pairs older regulars with the younger people they invited, and a share of the newcomers sign up for the regular rota afterwards.
2. Newcomers who enjoyed the day are asked to join a regular slot before they leave, and the coordinator records who says yes.
3. Every invited newcomer passes the short food-safety briefing that morning, before going to a station.
4. Each of the 30 over-65 regulars has a younger relative or neighbour who is eligible to volunteer and has agreed to come.
5. The coordinator has announced the first generations Saturday and asked the 30 regulars to bring someone.
6. Today: the coordinator picks a date and drafts the invitation, using one week's spare hours.

**Broken link:** Step 4. Framed as "bring a grandchild", many of the people the 30 over-65 regulars bring will be teenagers, and the brief requires every volunteer to be over 18.
**Possible fix:** Call it "bring a younger adult", say in the invitation that the guest must be 18 or over (an adult grandchild, child, nephew, niece or neighbour), and check ages at the sign-in desk before the briefing.

### 3. Pedal parcels

**Verdict:** ✅ Adherent and walkable
**Adherence:** Yes. A Saturday delivery role recruited through a cycling club brings in a block of active volunteers who are mostly under 50, which counts directly towards the 40 and the under-50 third.
**Backward chain:**

1. Every Saturday club riders and cargo-bike owners deliver parcels to housebound households along fixed routes, and the van is free for supermarket pickups.
2. Riders who are over 18 have had the food-safety briefing and carry insulated bags, so chilled items stay safe on the ride.
3. A short list of housebound households who want home delivery has their consent to share addresses with riders, grouped into a few fixed routes from the warehouse.
4. A local cycling club has agreed to adopt the deliveries as a regular Saturday club ride, and the insulated bags have been bought (a few hundred euros of the €3,000).
5. The coordinator has asked recipients at the Saturday handout who knows a housebound neighbour or relative, and has met the club captain.
6. Today: the coordinator finds a local cycling club and messages its captain, and puts a "do you know someone who can't get here?" note on the handout table. Both fit in this week's four spare hours.

### 4. University service-learning partner

**Verdict:** 🔧 Adherent, link to fix
**Adherence:** Yes. A rolling cohort of students each term is a steady supply of adult volunteers under 30, and with some staying on after their module it can keep the rota at 40 with well over a third under 50.
**Backward chain:**

1. Each term a new group of students works a set number of Saturdays for course credit, and some carry on after the module ends.
2. The coordinator confirms each student's hours, and the university counts them towards the module.
3. Second Shelf is an approved placement provider: it has a signed placement agreement, a risk assessment, and a named supervisor who completes the university's placement paperwork and assessment forms for every student, every term.
4. A module leader at a nearby university or college has agreed that Saturday-only food-bank work meets the module's hour requirements.
5. Today: the coordinator emails the volunteering or service-learning office of the nearest university or college, within this week's spare hours.

**Broken link:** Step 3. Being a placement provider for social-work or nutrition modules usually means a named supervisor who supervises and assesses each student and fills in termly forms, often on weekdays. That is more than the coordinator's four spare hours a week, and it contradicts the card's claim that the idea costs no coordinator time.
**Possible fix:** Aim only at volunteering-credit or service-learning modules where students log their own hours and the university does the assessing. The coordinator's only task is to countersign a Saturday sign-in sheet once a month, and the partnership starts with one cohort per term.
